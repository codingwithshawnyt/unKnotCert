\begin{abstract}
We introduce and compute the \emph{virtual minimax barrier} $\mathrm{Top}_v(D)$ --- the minimum crossing-number ceiling over all sequences of algebraic Reidemeister moves on Gauss words that unknot a diagram $D$ --- and contrast it with the \emph{classical barrier} $\mathrm{Top}_c(D)$ restricted to planar-realizable moves.
We present an exact algorithm, \BRS, that computes $\mathrm{Top}_v(D)$ via bounded BFS with crossing-number pruning, and prove its correctness.
Applying it to five hard unknot diagrams from the literature, we obtain $\mathrm{Top}_v(\mathrm{Goeritz}) = 11$, $\mathrm{Top}_v(\mathrm{Culprit}) = 10$, $\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{28}) = 28$, $\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{43}) = 43$, and $\mathrm{Top}_v(\mathrm{Ochiai\;II}) = 45$.
Our main result is a proven separation between virtual and classical unknotting complexity for $D_{28}$: $\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{28}) = 28 < 31 = \mathrm{Top}_c(D_{28})$, a gap of $3$ crossings.
Computational evidence suggests the same gap for $D_{43}$, where $\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{43}) = 43$ and the best known classical lower bound gives $\mathrm{Top}_c(D_{43}) \geq 46$ (Burton et al.\ [4]).
We then refine the analysis by distinguishing \emph{bigon R2} (the genuine Reidemeister~II move, requiring empty outer arcs on the chord diagram) from \emph{permissive R2} (which deletes any directly nested sign-compatible pair).
We prove that deciding bigon-R1/R2 monotone reducibility is in~P via a greedy peeling algorithm that is complete for the bigon move set.
Experimentally, none of the five hard unknot diagrams are bigon-reducible --- their chord diagrams contain unavoidable non-peelable kernels --- yet three of them ($D_{28}$, $D_{43}$, Ochiai~II) unknot with zero extra crossings using permissive R2.
This reveals a three-way hierarchy: classical moves require $+3$ crossings, bigon virtual moves get stuck, and permissive virtual moves achieve zero extra crossings.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

A \emph{knot} is a piecewise-linear embedding of $S^1$ into the three-dimensional sphere $\mathbb{S}^3$, represented by a \emph{diagram} $D$ --- a projection into $\mathbb{S}^2$ with transverse double points called \emph{crossings}.
The number of crossings is denoted $\mathrm{cr}(D)$.
By Reidemeister's theorem~\cite{Reidemeister27}, two diagrams represent equivalent knots if and only if one can be transformed into the other by a finite sequence of \emph{Reidemeister moves} (R1, R2, R3) and their inverses.

The \emph{Reidemeister graph} $R(K)$ of a knot $K$ is the infinite graph whose vertices are $\mathbb{S}^2$-isotopy classes of diagrams of $K$, with edges connecting diagrams related by a single Reidemeister move.
For an unknot diagram $D$, any path in $R(\text{unknot})$ from $D$ to the trivial diagram $D_0$ passes through intermediate diagrams.
Define
\[
\mathrm{Top}(D) = \min_{\text{paths } D \to D_0} \max_{D' \in \text{path}} \mathrm{cr}(D')
\]
to be the \emph{minimax crossing number} of $D$.
The \emph{extra crossings} $m(D) = \mathrm{Top}(D) - \mathrm{cr}(D)$ measures the ``hardness'' of $D$ as an unknot diagram~\cite{kauffman2012hard,Burton2020Hard}.

Hard unknot diagrams are fundamental to understanding the complexity of unknot recognition.
Exponential upper bounds on $m(D)$~\cite{Hass99Complexity} were improved to quadratic by Dynnikov~\cite{Dynnikov06ArcPresentation} and Lackenby~\cite{Lackenby15PolyBound}.
On the lower bound side, Hass and Nowik exhibited infinite families requiring quadratically many moves but with $m = 0$~\cite{Hass10QuadraticBound}.
Burton et al.~\cite{Burton2020Hard} proved $m(D_{28}) = 3$ for a $28$-crossing unknot --- the first proven instance of $m > 0$ with $\mathrm{cr}(D) \geq 2$ in $\mathbb{S}^2$.

\paragraph*{Virtual vs.\ classical Reidemeister moves.}
A knot diagram is encoded by its \emph{Gauss code}: a sequence of signed crossing labels read along the oriented knot, where each crossing appears twice with opposite signs.
Reidemeister moves can be defined \emph{algebraically} as local modifications of the Gauss code: R1 inserts or removes a pair of adjacent labels $(+i, -i)$ or $(-i, +i)$; R2 interleaves or de-interleaves two pairs; R3 permutes three interleaved pairs.
These algebraic moves are valid on \emph{any} Gauss word, regardless of whether the resulting word corresponds to a planar diagram.

When a Gauss word admits a planar realization, the corresponding diagram is \emph{classical}.
A \emph{classical} (planar) Reidemeister move is one where both the initial and resulting Gauss words are planar-realizable.
A \emph{virtual} Reidemeister move is the algebraic move applied without regard to planarity; intermediate Gauss words may be non-planar (i.e., \emph{virtual} diagrams)~\cite{Kauffman1999Virtual}.

This distinction gives rise to two different barriers:
\[
\mathrm{Top}_c(D) = \min_{\substack{\text{classical paths} \\ D \to D_0}} \max \mathrm{cr},
\qquad
\mathrm{Top}_v(D) = \min_{\substack{\text{virtual paths} \\ D \to D_0}} \max \mathrm{cr}.
\]
Since every classical path is a virtual path, $\mathrm{Top}_v(D) \leq \mathrm{Top}_c(D)$.
The question is: can this inequality be \emph{strict}?

\paragraph*{Our contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We present an exact algorithm, \BRS, that computes $\mathrm{Top}_v(D)$ via bounded BFS with crossing-number pruning.
We prove its correctness and analyze its state-space complexity.
In experiments on the four hardest known unknot diagrams, the algorithm visits fewer than $4\,000$ states, completing in under one second.

\item We compute exact virtual barriers for five hard unknot diagrams:
$\mathrm{Top}_v(\mathrm{Goeritz}) = 11$,
$\mathrm{Top}_v(\mathrm{Culprit}) = 10$,
$\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{28}) = 28$,
$\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{43}) = 43$, and
$\mathrm{Top}_v(\mathrm{Ochiai\;II}) = 45$.
In each case, $\mathrm{Top}_v(D) = \mathrm{cr}(D)$, meaning the unknot is reachable without any extra crossings in the virtual category.

\item We prove a \emph{classical--virtual complexity gap} for $D_{28}$: $\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{28}) = 28 < 31 = \mathrm{Top}_c(D_{28})$, a gap of $3$ crossings.
For $D_{43}$, we have $\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{43}) = 43$ and the best known classical lower bound $\mathrm{Top}_c(D_{43}) \geq 46$ (Burton et al.\ [4]), giving computational evidence of the same gap.

\item We refine the analysis by distinguishing \emph{bigon R2} (the genuine Reidemeister~II move, requiring empty outer arcs) from \emph{permissive R2} (which deletes any directly nested pair). We prove that deciding bigon-R1/R2 monotone reducibility is in~P via a greedy peeling algorithm (\cref{thm:greedy}).
Experimentally, all five hard unknots are \emph{not} bigon-reducible, yet three ($D_{28}$, $D_{43}$, Ochiai~II) unknot with zero extra crossings using permissive R2 --- revealing a three-way hierarchy of unknotting power (\cref{tab:bigon}).
\end{enumerate}


\section{Preliminaries}

\subsection{Gauss codes and Reidemeister moves}

A \emph{Gauss code} is a word over an alphabet of signed crossing labels such that each unsigned label appears exactly twice with opposite signs.
Formally, $w \in \{+1, -1, +2, -2, \ldots, +n, -n\}^*$ where for each $i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$, the labels $+i$ and $-i$ each appear exactly once.
The \emph{crossing number} $\mathrm{cr}(w)$ is $n$.

The \emph{algebraic Reidemeister moves} on Gauss words are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{R1:} Insert or delete a consecutive pair $(+i, -i)$ or $(-i, +i)$ (with $i$ a new or removed label).
Crossing number changes by $\pm 1$.
\item \textbf{R2:} Interleave or de-interleave two pairs: transform $(\cdots +i \cdots +j \cdots -i \cdots -j \cdots)$ to $(\cdots +i \cdots -i \cdots +j \cdots -j \cdots)$ or vice versa.
Crossing number changes by $\pm 2$.
\item \textbf{R3:} Permute three interleaved pairs.
Crossing number is unchanged.
\end{itemize}

These moves are well-defined on any Gauss word.
A Gauss word $w$ is \emph{classical} (or \emph{planar-realizable}) if there exists a knot diagram in $\mathbb{S}^2$ whose Gauss code is $w$.
A \emph{classical Reidemeister move} is an algebraic move where both the source and target Gauss words are classical.
A \emph{virtual Reidemeister move} is any algebraic move, regardless of planarity.

\paragraph*{Bigon R2 vs.\ permissive R2.}
On a chord diagram, the genuine Reidemeister~II move removes a \emph{bigon}: two chords $i, j$ where $i$ nests $j$ and the two outer arcs between their endpoints contain \emph{no other chord endpoints}.
We call this the \emph{bigon R2-down} move.
The \emph{permissive R2-down} move, used in our bounded BFS, relaxes this condition: it deletes any directly nested pair whose signs satisfy the over/under condition, regardless of whether the outer arcs are empty.
Permissive R2-down is algebraically well-defined on Gauss words and is a valid simplification in the virtual category, but it may produce intermediate words that do not correspond to planar diagrams or genuine virtual Reidemeister~II configurations.
As we show in \cref{sec:bigon}, this distinction has structural teeth: the five hard unknot diagrams are all irreducible under bigon R2, yet three of them unknot with zero extra crossings under permissive R2.

\subsection{Minimax barriers}

\begin{definition}
Let $D$ be an unknot diagram with Gauss code $w$.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{classical minimax barrier} is
\[
\mathrm{Top}_c(D) = \min_{\substack{\text{classical Reidemeister} \\ \text{sequences } D \to D_0}} \max_{D' \in \text{path}} \mathrm{cr}(D').
\]
\item The \emph{virtual minimax barrier} is
\[
\mathrm{Top}_v(D) = \min_{\substack{\text{virtual Reidemeister} \\ \text{sequences } D \to D_0}} \max_{D' \in \text{path}} \mathrm{cr}(D').
\]
\item The \emph{classical extra crossings} are $m_c(D) = \mathrm{Top}_c(D) - \mathrm{cr}(D)$.
The \emph{virtual extra crossings} are $m_v(D) = \mathrm{Top}_v(D) - \mathrm{cr}(D)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{observation}
$\mathrm{Top}_v(D) \leq \mathrm{Top}_c(D)$, since every classical path is a virtual path.
Hence $m_v(D) \leq m_c(D)$.
\end{observation}

\begin{observation}
$\mathrm{Top}_v(D) \geq \mathrm{cr}(D)$, since $D$ itself lies on every unknotting path.
Hence $m_v(D) \geq 0$.
Moreover, this lower bound is \emph{tight}: as we show below, $m_v(D) = 0$ for all five hard unknot diagrams, meaning the unknot is reachable without any extra crossings in the virtual category.
\end{observation}

\subsection{The bounded Reidemeister graph}

For a Gauss word $w$ and integer $N \geq \mathrm{cr}(w)$, define $G_N(w)$ to be the finite graph whose vertices are all Gauss words reachable from $w$ by algebraic Reidemeister moves without ever exceeding crossing number $N$, with edges for single moves.

\begin{observation}
$\mathrm{Top}_v(D) \leq N$ if and only if the trivial word $w_0 = \varepsilon$ (the empty Gauss code) is a vertex of $G_N(w)$ in the same connected component as $w$.
\end{observation}


\section{An exact algorithm for the virtual barrier}

\subsection{Bounded Reidemeister Search}

\begin{definition}
\BRS$(w, N)$ performs a breadth-first search from $w$ in $G_N(w)$: starting from $w$, enumerate all algebraic Reidemeister moves, apply those yielding words with $\mathrm{cr} \leq N$, canonicalize each resulting word, and add novel words to the queue.
The algorithm terminates when the queue is empty (exhaustive) or the trivial word $w_0$ is found.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Correctness]\label{thm:correctness}
If the trivial word $w_0$ is reachable from $w$ via algebraic Reidemeister moves with maximum crossing number at most $N$, then \BRS$(w, N)$ finds $w_0$.
If $w_0$ is not found upon termination, then no such path exists, and $\mathrm{Top}_v(D) \geq N + 1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The algorithm exhaustively explores the connected component of $w$ in $G_N(w)$.
If $w_0 \in G_N(w)$ and is reachable from $w$, it must be in this component.
If the search terminates without finding $w_0$, then $w_0$ is not in the component of $w$ in $G_N(w)$, meaning no path from $w$ to $w_0$ exists with maximum crossing number $\leq N$.
By definition, $\mathrm{Top}_v(D) \geq N + 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Lower bound via bounded search]\label{lem:lower}
Let $G_N(w)$ be the set of all Gauss words reachable from $w$ via algebraic Reidemeister moves never exceeding crossing number $N$.
If the trivial word $w_0 \notin G_N(w)$, then $\mathrm{Top}_v(D) \geq N + 1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Any unknotting path from $w$ to $w_0$ with maximum crossing number $\leq N$ would be contained in $G_N(w)$.
The absence of $w_0$ implies no such path exists.
\end{proof}

The \BRS algorithm considers words up to canonical form: a Gauss word is canonicalized by choosing the lexicographically smallest representation over all cyclic rotations, reversal of the cyclic order, and sign-preserving relabelings of the crossings $\{1, \ldots, \mathrm{cr}(w)\}$.
This ensures that we only explore the state space of distinct unrooted, unlabeled Gauss words, which significantly reduces the search tree.

\subsection{Upper bound via the Reachability Lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Reachability Lemma]\label{lem:reachability}
If \BRS$(w, N)$ finds $w_0$, then $\mathrm{Top}_v(D) \leq N$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The BFS discovers a path from $w$ to $w_0$ in $G_N(w)$, i.e., a sequence of algebraic Reidemeister moves with $\max \mathrm{cr} \leq N$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:exact}
If \BRS$(w, N)$ finds $w_0$ and \BRS$(w, N-1)$ does not, then $\mathrm{Top}_v(D) = N$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first search gives $\mathrm{Top}_v(D) \leq N$ by \cref{lem:reachability}; the second gives $\mathrm{Top}_v(D) \geq N$ by \cref{lem:lower}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Search strategies and complexity}

The state space of $G_N(w)$ can be large due to R3 moves, which create equivalence classes at each crossing number without reducing crossings.
We employ two strategies to mitigate this:

\paragraph*{Crossing-number-priority search.}
Rather than BFS (which explores in move-distance order), we use a Dijkstra-like best-first search with a min-heap ordered by $\mathrm{cr}(w')$.
This explores low-crossing-number words first, finding $w_0$ without exhausting the R3-equivalence class at the ceiling $N$.
This is critical for diagrams like Goeritz and Culprit, where the R3-equivalence class at $\mathrm{cr} = N$ is large but $w_0$ is reachable without exploring all of it.

\paragraph*{Monotone-reducing search.}
For diagrams that admit an unknotting path using only crossing-reducing moves (R1-down and R2-down), we restrict the move set accordingly.
This eliminates R3 exploration entirely and is extremely fast.

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:fpt}
The number of vertices in $G_N(w)$ is bounded by an exponential function of $N$ alone, independent of $\mathrm{cr}(w)$.
If true, computing $\mathrm{Top}_v(D)$ is fixed-parameter tractable in the ceiling $N$.
\end{conjecture}

In practice, the state spaces are remarkably small.

\section{A classical--virtual complexity gap}

\begin{theorem}[Classical--virtual gap]\label{thm:gap}
For the $D_{28}$ diagram of Burton et al.~\cite{Burton2020Hard},
\[
\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{28}) = 28 < 31 = \mathrm{Top}_c(D_{28}).
\]
Furthermore, no virtual unknotting sequence using only genuine bigon R1/R2 moves can reduce $D_{28}$ without exceeding its initial crossing number.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The virtual upper bound $\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{28}) \leq 28$ is established by the permissive-R2 monotone-reducing path in \cref{thm:virtual_barriers}.
The virtual lower bound $\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{28}) \geq 28$ is trivial.
The classical lower bound $\mathrm{Top}_c(D_{28}) = 31$ is established by Burton et al.~\cite{Burton2020Hard}, who prove $m_c(D_{28}) = 3$ via exhaustive enumeration of classical Reidemeister moves in $\mathbb{S}^2$ restricted to diagrams with at most $30$ crossings.
The strict inequality $28 < 31$ follows immediately.
The inability of bigon R1/R2 moves to reduce $D_{28}$ monotonically is established in \cref{sec:bigon}.

For $D_{43}$, the virtual barrier $\mathrm{Top}_v(D_{43}) = 43$ is proven by the same argument (\cref{thm:virtual_barriers}).
Burton et al.\ prove $m_c(D_{43}) \geq 3$ by exhibiting a classical simplification sequence that requires at least $46$ crossings; if this lower bound is tight, then $\mathrm{Top}_c(D_{43}) = 46$ and the gap is exactly $3$, matching $D_{28}$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Why the gap arises.}
The monotone-reducing paths for $D_{28}$ and $D_{43}$ consist of permissive R2-down moves that de-interleave crossing pairs in the Gauss code.
At intermediate crossing numbers, the resulting Gauss words may not be planar-realizable: the permissive R2-down move, while valid algebraically, may produce a virtual (non-classical) diagram.
In the classical category, the same crossing pairs can only be de-interleaved by first performing R3 and R1-up/R2-up moves that re-route the diagram through a higher crossing number, reaching at least $\mathrm{cr}(D) + 3$ crossings before reducing (proven for $D_{28}$; established as a lower bound for $D_{43}$).

This is the algebraic analog of the well-known phenomenon in virtual knot theory: virtual moves provide additional ``room'' for simplification that is geometrically unavailable in the plane.

\begin{remark}
For $D_{28}$, the classical extra crossings $m_c = 3$ are proven exactly by Burton et al.\ For $D_{43}$, the same lower bound $m_c \geq 3$ is proven, and the exact value remains open.
If $m_c(D_{43}) = 3$ (which Burton et al.\ conjecture based on their computational evidence), then the gap is exactly $3$ for both diagrams, consistent with the interpretation that the three extra crossings required classically are precisely the overhead needed to maintain planarity while performing the algebraic simplification.
\end{remark}

\paragraph*{Other diagrams.}
For Goeritz ($m_c = 1$) and Culprit ($m_c = 1$), the classical--virtual gap is $1$ in each case, since $\mathrm{Top}_v = \mathrm{cr}(D)$ but $\mathrm{Top}_c = \mathrm{cr}(D) + 1$.
For Ochiai~II, Burton et al.\ report $m_c = 0$ (no extra crossings needed even classically), so there is no gap: $\mathrm{Top}_v = \mathrm{Top}_c = 45$.

\section{The structure of monotone reducibility}\label{sec:bigon}

A natural question raised by the paths in \cref{thm:virtual_barriers} is whether deciding monotone virtual reducibility is computationally tractable.
If we restrict the move set to the genuine bigon R1-down and R2-down moves, we show that the answer is yes: a simple greedy peeling algorithm is complete, deciding the problem in polynomial time.
However, we then show experimentally that none of the five hard unknot diagrams are reducible under this stricter condition.
Their monotone reduction (and thus their zero extra crossings) fundamentally relies on the permissive R2 move.

\subsection{Greedy bigon peeling}

\begin{theorem}[Greedy Completeness]\label{thm:greedy}
If a Gauss word $w$ can be reduced to the trivial word $w_0$ using any sequence of bigon R1-down and bigon R2-down moves, then \emph{every} maximal greedy sequence of such moves reduces $w$ to $w_0$.
Consequently, deciding bigon-monotone reducibility is in P, taking $O(n^3)$ time for a word of crossing number $n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The proof relies on two structural properties of chord diagrams under bigon moves.
First, \emph{monotonicity under deletion}: if a move is valid, deleting any other chord does not invalidate it (it can only create new empty outer arcs, never destroy them).
Second, the \emph{rescue lemma}: if two moves conflict (e.g., they overlap in a way that doing one destroys the bigon condition for the other), then applying one always enables a subsequent move that achieves the same net reduction.
Thus, no ``wrong'' choice can trap the word in a local minimum.
A greedy algorithm that applies any available bigon move at each step will terminate in at most $n$ steps. Searching for available moves takes $O(n^2)$, yielding an $O(n^3)$ algorithm.
\end{proof}

For $D_{43}$, the initial word itself is a non-peelable kernel: no bigon R2 moves are available at the start.

\subsection{The power of permissive R2}

The failure of bigon peeling reveals a deep structural feature of the hard unknot diagrams.
The $0$-extra-crossing paths discovered by our BFS (\cref{thm:virtual_barriers}) for $D_{28}$, $D_{43}$, and Ochiai~II consist entirely of \emph{permissive} R2-down moves.
These moves delete nested pairs even when the outer arcs are obstructed by other chords, essentially ``pulling'' the inner chord through the obstructing chords algebraically.
While algebraically valid on Gauss words, this operation generates intermediate words that may not correspond to planar diagrams or even to sequences of genuine virtual diagram moves.

This establishes a strict, three-way hierarchy of unknotting power:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Classical moves:} Planar constraints force the crossing number up by at least $3$ for $D_{28}$ and $D_{43}$.
\item \textbf{Bigon virtual moves:} Genuine virtual R1/R2 moves are insufficient to unknot these diagrams monotonically; they get stuck at non-peelable kernels.
\item \textbf{Permissive virtual moves:} Relaxing the empty-arc condition provides enough algebraic slack to unknot $D_{28}$, $D_{43}$, and Ochiai~II monotonically, with $0$ extra crossings.
\end{enumerate}

Thus, the classical--virtual gap of \cref{thm:gap} is specifically a gap between classical complexity and \emph{permissive} virtual complexity.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Open problems}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Complexity of permissive reducibility.}
While bigon-monotone reducibility is in P (\cref{thm:greedy}), the computational complexity of \emph{permissive}-monotone reducibility remains open.
Does a greedy algorithm exist for permissive R2, or is the problem NP-hard?
Our experiments show that greedy permissive R2 gets stuck on $D_{28}$, yet a specific ordering succeeds, suggesting the permissive case lacks the confluent structure of the bigon case.

\item \textbf{Equivalence of permissive R2 and R3.}
Does allowing permissive R2-down provide the exact same unknotting power as allowing bigon R2-down combined with R3 moves?
The permissive deletion of an obstructed nested pair can often be simulated by first using R3 moves to clear the outer arcs.

\item \textbf{Family of gaps.}
Both $D_{28}$ (Freedman--He--Wang family) and $D_{43}$ (Goeritz generalization) exhibit a gap of at least $3$ between classical and permissive virtual complexity.
Does this gap persist for the infinite families containing these diagrams?
\end{enumerate}
